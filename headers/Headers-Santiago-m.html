Beetles


External Morphology

Head

The head of a beetle is typically hardened (sclerotized) and sometimes very large, with mouthparts pointing either forward or downward. It features compound eyes that show great variety and adaptability; for example, whirligig beetles have split eyes for vision above and below the water. Many beetles have notched eyes, and some have eyes that are fully divided (like certain Longhorn beetles and weevils). Additionally, a few beetles have simple eyes called ocelli located on the back of the head, which are more common in larvae. The compound eyes' structure is often adapted depending on whether the species is active during the day (diurnal), night (nocturnal), or twilight (crepuscular).

Mouthparts

Beetles breathe through a tracheal system, taking in air via spiracles. Diving beetles carry an air bubble trapped under their elytra or on their body, which acts as a physical gill by allowing oxygen to diffuse from water into the bubble while carbon dioxide diffuses out. The bubble’s nitrogen remains stable, helping maintain respiration, and beetles surface occasionally to refresh the air supply.

Thorax

The beetle's thorax is divided into two main parts: the prothorax and the pterothorax (a fusion of the meso- and metathorax). All three pairs of legs and both pairs of wings originate from the thorax. Although beetles appear to have three clear body sections from above, the middle section (the pronotum) is only the front part of the thorax, with the back part hidden by the wings. The abdomen is located behind the thorax and shows clearer segmentation.

Protborax and Plerothorax

In beetles, the protothorax refers to the first segment of the thorax, which is located right behind the head. It typically bears the first pair of legs and plays a crucial role in protecting the beetle’s vital organs and supporting movement. The plerothorax encompasses the entire thoracic region, including the prothorax, mesothorax, and metathorax segments. This combined thoracic structure supports all three pairs of legs and the wings, enabling beetles to walk, fly, and perform other essential functions. Together, these thoracic parts provide structural support and mobility critical for the beetle’s survival.

Legs

Beetle legs end in 2 to 5 small segments called tarsi, usually with one pair of claws on the last segment. While most beetles use their legs for walking, some have specialized adaptations: aquatic beetles often have hairy legs for swimming; male diving beetles have suctorial cups on their forelegs to hold females; some beetles have widened, spined legs for digging; and others, like flea beetles, have enlarged hind legs for jumping.

Wings

Beetles’ forewings form hard protective covers called elytra that shield the hindwings, which are used for flight. The elytra must be lifted to allow the hindwings to move. Some beetles, like soldier beetles, have soft elytra, while others have fragile wings that break to release defensive chemicals. Beetle flight wings are veined and fold neatly under the elytra. Some beetles have lost the ability to fly, often having fused elytra forming a solid shield. In some species, both wings and elytra are lost entirely, such as female glow-worms. Also, having wings and elytra doesn’t always mean the beetle will fly, as some prefer walking between habitats.

Abdomen

The beetle abdomen, located behind the metathorax, consists of segmented rings with breathing holes called spiracles. It is made up of three main parts: the tergum (usually soft and hidden), the pleura (small and containing spiracles), and the visible sternum. The abdomen generally lacks appendages, though some species have specialized sternal lobes.
